c. 1207 BCE–638 CE

Jewish presence in the land, from about 1200 BCE to 638 CE

Written evidence places the Jewish people in the land more than a thousand years before Jesus and about 1,800 years before the rise of Islam.

Source: Merneptah Stele (Cairo Museum), primary inscription · Tel Dan inscription: Biran and Naveh, Israel Exploration Journal 43 (1993) · Babylonian Chronicle ABC 5 (BM 21946, British Museum) · Josephus, The Jewish War (primary account)

The evidence comes from independent sources, not only from the Bible: (1) the Merneptah Stele (Egypt, c. 1207 BCE) names “Israel” as a people in Canaan; (2) the Tel Dan inscription (9th century BCE) mentions a “House of David”; (3) Hebrew inscriptions from the 8th to 6th centuries BCE, including the Siloam tunnel inscription in Jerusalem and the Lachish letters, show Hebrew in daily use; (4) the Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946) records Nebuchadnezzar’s capture of “the city of Judah” on 16 March 597 BCE, and Babylonian ration tablets from about 592 BCE name “Yaukin, king of Judah”; (5) the return in the Persian period (538 BCE) and the Second Temple (c. 516 BCE); (6) Hasmonean coins and the independent kingdom of 140–63 BCE; (7) Josephus’s account of the war of 66–70 CE and the Arch of Titus in Rome; (8) the Bar Kokhba letters and coins (132–136 CE); and (9) after 135 CE, the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) and the Jerusalem Talmud (c. 400 CE), edited in the Galilee, and the synagogues of the Byzantine period. All of this evidence predates the Arab conquest of Jerusalem in 638 CE and the rise of Islam in the 7th century.

Other perspectives: Scholars debate how the Israelites first emerged (conquest, gradual settlement or local development), the scale of the united monarchy under David and Solomon, and the exact date and scale of the later exile. The 586 BCE destruction of Jerusalem is attested by the Bible and by archaeology; the Babylonian Chronicle for those years has not survived. None of these debates affects the main point: a Jewish presence in the land is attested in every period covered here.

Further reading (books and articles):
  • Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman, The Bible Unearthed (Free Press, 2001)
  • Seth Schwartz, Imperialism and Jewish Society, 200 B.C.E. to 640 C.E. (Princeton University Press, 2001)
  • Lee I. Levine, Jerusalem: Portrait of the City in the Second Temple Period (Jewish Publication Society, 2002)
  • Donald J. Wiseman, Chronicles of Chaldaean Kings (626–556 B.C.) in the British Museum (British Museum, 1956)

c. 1000–135 BCE

Hebrew: the language of the land

Hebrew was the language of the Israelites and Judeans.

Source: Inscriptions: Gezer calendar, Siloam inscription (Israel Museum / Istanbul), Lachish letters (British Museum), primary artifacts

Written Hebrew is attested from about the 10th century BCE (the Gezer calendar and the Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon, though scholars debate whether their language is Hebrew or a close Canaanite dialect) and securely later: the Samaria ostraca (8th century), the Siloam tunnel inscription (c. 700 BCE), the Lachish letters (c. 588 BCE) and the Hebrew Bible itself. Hebrew also appears in the Dead Sea Scrolls and in the Bar Kokhba letters (c. 135 CE). The word “Jew” comes from “Judean” (Yehudi), a member of the people of Judah, who spoke Hebrew. About 3,000 years of attested Hebrew is well supported; 3,500 is not, since older Canaanite inscriptions cannot be identified as Hebrew.

Whether the very earliest inscriptions are Hebrew or Canaanite is debated; inscriptions from the 8th century on are securely Hebrew.

c. 200–1948

When Hebrew was spoken, and when it was revived

Hebrew is usually held to have faded as an everyday spoken language between about 200 and 400 CE, but it stayed alive in prayer, law, poetry, philosophy and correspondence across the diaspora.

Source: Jack Fellman, The Revival of a Classical Tongue (Mouton, 1973)

In the 1880s Eliezer Ben-Yehuda began using it as a home language in Jerusalem, and under the British Mandate (1922) it was one of three official languages. Scholars such as Ghil’ad Zuckermann argue that modern Israeli Hebrew is a hybrid language; the mainstream view sees it as a direct continuation.

Estimates of when speaking ceased vary among scholars.

Further reading (books and articles):
  • Jack Fellman, The Revival of a Classical Tongue: Eliezer Ben-Yehuda and the Modern Hebrew Language (Mouton, 1973)
  • Angel Sáenz-Badillos, A History of the Hebrew Language (Cambridge University Press, 1993)
  • Ghil’ad Zuckermann, Israelit Safa Yafa / Revivalistics (Oxford University Press, 2020), for the hybrid view

Kingdoms of Israel and Judah

Two related kingdoms existed in the land in the first millennium BCE: Israel in the north (capital Samaria) and Judah in the south (capital Jerusalem).

Source: Tel Dan inscription: Biran and Naveh, Israel Exploration Journal 43 (1993)

538–516 BCE

Return and the Second Temple

After Persia conquered Babylon in 539 BCE, Cyrus allowed exiled peoples to return home. Some Judeans returned and a Second Temple was dedicated around 516 BCE.

Source: Lester L. Grabbe, A History of the Jews and Judaism in the Second Temple Period (T&T Clark, 2004)

Many Jews stayed in Babylon, so a diaspora and a homeland existed side by side from this time.

The Cyrus Cylinder does not mention Judea; the connection comes from Ezra and wider Persian policy.

c. 200–640 CE

Jewish life continues in the land

Jews did not disappear from the land after 70 or 135 CE.

Source: Seth Schwartz, Imperialism and Jewish Society, 200 B.C.E. to 640 C.E. (Princeton University Press, 2001)

The Galilee became the center of Jewish learning: the Mishnah was edited around 200 CE and the Jerusalem Talmud around 400 CE, both in the land. At the same time, large Jewish communities lived in Babylonia, Egypt and across the Roman world. “Exile” was gradual and partial, not a single event.

638–1516

Jewish presence in the land, 638–1516: Arab, Crusader and Mamluk rule

Jewish communities remained in the land through the early Muslim, Crusader and Mamluk periods, mainly in Tiberias, Jerusalem, Ramla, Safed, Hebron and the Galilee, though often small, poor and at times persecuted.

Source: Moshe Gil, A History of Palestine, 634–1099 (Cambridge University Press, 1992) · Joshua Prawer, The History of the Jews in the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem (Oxford University Press, 1988) · Cairo Genizah letters (primary documents) · Nahmanides, letter from Jerusalem, 1267 (primary)

(1) After the Arab conquest of 638, Jewish communities continued in Tiberias, Jerusalem, Ramla, Tyre and the Galilee. Tiberias was the home of the Masoretes, the scholars who fixed the vowel and cantillation signs of the Hebrew Bible there between the 8th and 10th centuries, and the academy (yeshiva) of the land of Israel was based first in Tiberias and later in Jerusalem. The Cairo Genizah preserves many letters from these communities of the 10th and 11th centuries. (2) The First Crusade (1099) brought massacres of Jews in Jerusalem and, in the following years, in Haifa and other coastal towns; the Crusaders barred Jews from living in Jerusalem, but Jewish communities continued in the Galilee and in some coastal towns. (3) After Saladin retook Jerusalem in 1187, Jews were again allowed to settle there. In 1267 Nahmanides (the Ramban), arriving from Spain, reorganized a Jewish community in Jerusalem and founded a synagogue; he spent his last years in Acre. (4) Under the Mamluks (1260/1291–1516) Jewish pilgrims and immigrants kept arriving, and in 1488 Obadiah of Bertinoro described about 70 poor Jewish families in Jerusalem. (5) After 1492, exiles from Spain strengthened the communities of Safed and Jerusalem (see the next entry).

Other perspectives: Other perspectives: Jewish communities in this period were small and often poor, and the population of the land was overwhelmingly Muslim and Christian. Estimates of Jewish numbers before the 19th century are rough and come from travelers’ accounts and community letters. This entry documents continuity of presence, not a majority.

1517–1948

Jewish presence in the land, 1517–1948: Ottoman and British rule

Jews lived in the land throughout Ottoman and British rule, in the four holy cities of Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias and, from the 1880s, in new farming villages and towns; by the end of 1946 they numbered about 608,000, roughly 31% of the population.

Source: Jewish Virtual Library, “Population of Jerusalem, 1844–2009” (after Oesterreicher and Sinai, 1974) · Ottoman records of 1878 (via PalQuest) · Report on Palestine Administration, 1922 (census results, UN Digital Library) · Palestine Government estimate of 31 December 1946, House of Commons written answer, 19 December 1947 (Hansard) · Abraham David, To Come to the Land (University of Alabama Press, 1999)

(1) After 1492 Safed became a major center of Jewish learning: Joseph Karo wrote the Shulchan Arukh there (printed in 1565) and Isaac Luria taught Kabbalah; sources put the town’s Jewish population at about 10,000 by the mid-16th century, out of roughly 5,000 Jews in the whole country in the 1530s. Tiberias was refounded as a Jewish town in the 1560s under the patronage of Doña Gracia Mendes and Joseph Nasi. (2) Under the Ottomans (1517–1917) Jews remained in Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias. A table in the Jewish Virtual Library, drawing on Oesterreicher and Sinai (1974), gives for Jerusalem 7,120 Jews out of 15,510 residents in 1844; 12,000 of 25,030 in 1876; and 28,112 of 45,420 in 1896. Estimates conflict on whether Jews or Muslims were the largest group before the 1880s, and the city’s boundaries changed. (3) Ottoman records of 1878 count about 15,000 Jews among roughly 462,000 inhabitants of three districts (about 3%), so Jews were a small minority of the country as a whole. (4) From 1882 the First Aliyah and later waves founded villages and towns (see the entry on how Zionism began); estimates for 1914 range from about 60,000 to about 94,000 Jews. (5) Under the British Mandate the Jewish population rose from 83,794 (11%) in the census of 1922 to about 608,000 (about 31%) in the Palestine Government’s estimate for 31 December 1946. In 1929 the long-established Jewish community of Hebron, about 800 people, was attacked (67 killed) and the survivors were evacuated by the British authorities. (6) In May 1948 the Jewish Quarter of Jerusalem’s Old City fell to Jordan’s Arab Legion and its residents were evacuated; Jews were barred from the Old City until 1967.

Other perspectives: Other perspectives: Palestinian historians such as Rashid Khalidi stress that for most of this period the large majority of the population was Arab, and that the rapid growth of the Jewish population after 1882 came mainly from immigration, which is the central point of dispute in the conflict. Figures for Jerusalem before 1900 are estimates and differ between sources.

Further reading (books and articles):
  • Abraham David, To Come to the Land: Immigration and Settlement in 16th-Century Eretz-Israel (University of Alabama Press, 1999)
  • Justin McCarthy, The Population of Palestine (Columbia University Press, 1990)
  • Alexander Schölch, Palestine in Transformation, 1856–1882 (Institute for Palestine Studies, 1993)

1862–1917

How Zionism began, 1862–1917

Modern Zionism took shape between the 1860s and 1917, decades before World War II, in response to pogroms, antisemitism and a much older attachment to Jerusalem.

Source: Moses Hess, Rome and Jerusalem (1862) · Leon Pinsker, Auto-Emancipation (1882) · Theodor Herzl, Der Judenstaat (1896) · Basel Program (1897) · Balfour Declaration (1917), Avalon Project

A short chronology. 1862: Moses Hess publishes Rome and Jerusalem and Rabbi Zvi Hirsch Kalischer publishes Seeking Zion, both calling for a Jewish national revival in the land. 1881–82: after the assassination of Tsar Alexander II, pogroms sweep the Russian Empire; Leon Pinsker publishes Auto-Emancipation; the Hibbat Zion societies form; the First Aliyah begins and founds villages such as Rishon LeZion (1882). 1894–96: the Dreyfus affair convinces Theodor Herzl, who publishes The Jewish State (1896). 1897: the First Zionist Congress in Basel adopts the Basel Program. 1904–14: the Second Aliyah, in the wake of the 1903 Kishinev pogrom, brings roughly 35,000–40,000 immigrants and founds Degania, the first kibbutz (1909), and Tel Aviv (1909). 1917: Britain issues the Balfour Declaration. 1922: the League of Nations confers the Mandate for Palestine. Every step in this list comes before the rise of Nazism in 1933 and before World War II.

Other perspectives: Why the movement arose is debated. Zionist writers stressed both attachment to the land and the need for refuge from persecution; critics, including some Palestinian historians, stress that the movement also developed within the European nationalism and imperial politics of its time and that the local Arab population was affected (see the claim on colonialism and the entry on “return”). The dates and texts in this list are not disputed.

Further reading (books and articles):
  • Shlomo Avineri, The Making of Modern Zionism (Basic Books, 1981)
  • Anita Shapira, Land and Power: The Zionist Resort to Force, 1881–1948 (Oxford University Press, 1992)
  • Walter Laqueur, A History of Zionism (Holt, Rinehart and Winston, 1972)
  • Derek Penslar, Theodor Herzl: The Charismatic Leader (Yale University Press, 2020)

⚖️ ContestedZionism

Is Zionism colonialism? The case that it is not

A debated label: Zionism is a national movement of return, not an empire’s colonization.

Source: Anita Shapira, Land and Power (Oxford University Press, 1992); Shlomo Avineri, The Making of Modern Zionism (Basic Books, 1981)

Colonialism normally means a state sending settlers to rule or exploit a foreign land for the benefit of a mother country. This site’s position is that Zionism does not fit that definition, for four reasons. (1) Origin: the Jewish people took shape in the land, a Jewish presence there is documented continuously, and the diaspora arose from exile and expulsion. Zionism described a return, and the 1922 Mandate recognized the “historical connection”. (2) No mother country: no state sent or financed the settlers as imperial agents; Jews came as refugees and volunteers, at times against British policy, which restricted immigration in 1939. (3) Motive: refuge from persecution and national revival, not extraction of resources or labor. (4) Ancestry: genetic studies indicate that most Jewish communities share Middle Eastern ancestry.

This is a reasoned position, not a settled fact. Scholars such as Shafir, Rodinson, Wolfe and Khalidi argue that Zionist leaders themselves used the term “colonization” (for example Herzl’s Jewish Colonial Trust, 1899), that land was purchased and settled in an inhabited country, and that the local population experienced it as settlement. Penslar notes colonial aspects but sees the label as incomplete. Genetics has limits: it does not decide national rights.

Method

  • Contested: a fact or interpretation the sources disagree on. Shown together with other views.
  • Common claims: one verdict from Not true, Misleading, Partly true, True, followed by an explanation.
  • Wikipedia is not a source. It appears only as a pointer to what it cites, and is labeled as such.
  • Books and articles under further reading are academic sources for deeper study. Verify page and edition before quoting.
  • Numbers are shown with a year and a source and do not update automatically.
